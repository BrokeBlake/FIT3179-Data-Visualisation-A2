Urban Sprawl: A Symptom of Modern Melbourne


Urban Sprawl refers to the widespread, most often unplanned expansion of residential areas at the edges of cities.

Melbourne, dubbed the “Doughnut City” in the late 1980s, has witnessed a rapid outward growth over the last half century, reshaping the way people live, work, and move.

The impact of urban sprawl remains a highly debated topic among planners, economists, and communities.

Those that Support Urban Sprawl highlight its role in making housing more affordable, providing families with larger homes and backyards, and aligning with consumer preferences towards low density living.

Critics, on the other hand, point to increased automobile dependency, inefficient land use, the loss of prime agricultural land, and boring, monotone architecture as major drawbacks.


Over the last half century, Melbourne's Suburban (2000-4000 people per square kilometre) and Periurban (500-2000) population has dropped from a total of 62% of the population to around 43% of the population.

This 18% drop has mainly been pickup up by Urban residential densities (4000-10000) and City Centre Densities (10000+), increasing from ~8% to 21% of the population.

Melbourne's Suburbs: Population Growth between 1975 - 2025

One of the primary reasons for this change, is that since the early 1990s, the Victorian Government has introduced several urban growth strategies designed to contain sprawl while promoting inner-city revitalisation.

Most famously, the 2001 initiaitive Melbourne 2030 sought to curb urban sprawl by introducing urban growth boundaries, reducing the proportion of low-density housing, and redirecting development.

These policies have had a significant impact on revitalising Melbourne's innermost suburbs. CBD-adjacent suburbs such as Melbourne, Southbank, and Carlton have seen over 1000% growth in the last 50 years.


The Docklands' population has increased from just 30 residents to over 33,000, more than a 100,000% increase.

While Outer-Suburban localities such as Point Cook and Pakenham have also exploded in population over the last half century, the heaviest impact these policies have had are on Inner Suburban localities such as Greensborough and Mitcham as examples, disincentivising people from moving into these areas.

How Melbourne's Population Density compares to Global cities

The City of Melbourne is not the only city suffering from Urban Sprawl. Other western cities have also experienced rapid growth in the last few decades.

Without proper regulation, these cities have sprawled outwards - with residents abandoning downtown residential areas in favour of lower density suburban living.

The cities of Miami, Los Angeles, and Detriot are some key examples of large cities which expansion has been left almost unchecked - with city centres often being referred to as "ghost towns".

However, there are several modern, highly developed cities Melbourne can learn from if it wishes for higher-density living.

The Japanese Muncipalities of Osaka and Tokyo both boast almost twice the population density of Melbourne.

European cities London, Paris, and Moscow reach around 7000 peolpe per square kilometre, with infrastructure designed around higher-density living.

Modern Chinese Megalopolises Beijing, Shanghai, and Guangzhou (including Hong Kong, Shenzen) boast almost 10,000 people per kilometre squared.
